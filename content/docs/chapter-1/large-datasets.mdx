---
title: 1.5 — Utilising large datasets
description: Why the size and variety of training data matter, with a peek at what GPT-3 learned from.
---

The performance of GPT- and BERT-like models isn't just about clever architecture —
it's also about the sheer **size and variety of the training text**. The data covers
billions of words across natural languages, programming languages, and countless topics.

## What GPT-3 was trained on

To make the scale concrete, here is the dataset behind GPT-3 (the foundation model for
the first version of ChatGPT):

| Dataset | What it is | Tokens | Share |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| **CommonCrawl (filtered)** | Web crawl data | 410 B | 60 % |
| **WebText2** | Web crawl data | 19 B | 22 % |
| **Books1** | Internet book corpus | 12 B | 8 % |
| **Books2** | Internet book corpus | 55 B | 8 % |
| **Wikipedia** | High-quality text | 3 B | 3 % |

A **token** ≈ a word or punctuation mark. We'll cover *how* you turn text into tokens
in Chapter 2.

### Quick notes on the numbers

- The tokens add up to ~509 B, but GPT-3 was actually trained on only 300 B of them. The
  authors never explained why.
- CommonCrawl alone is about **570 GB** on disk.
- Newer models like Meta's **LLaMA** added Arxiv papers (~92 GB) and StackExchange code
  Q&As (~78 GB) on top of web data.

> **Why this matters.** When a model sees so much text from so many places, it picks up
> syntax, semantics, and even a chunk of "world knowledge" for free. That's what makes
> these models so good at *generalising* to tasks they've never explicitly been trained
> on.

## Pretraining is expensive

Because pretraining needs huge data and huge compute, the bill for GPT-3-sized runs
lands in the millions of dollars (~**\$4.6 M** for GPT-3 in cloud credits).

## The good news for the rest of the chapter

We **won't** spend millions. Here's the plan:

1. We'll **write code** that does pretraining, and run it on a small dataset for
   learning purposes.
2. We'll also learn how to **load openly available model weights** so you can skip the
   expensive pretraining step entirely and jump straight to finetuning later on.

Either path is useful — writing it yourself teaches you the mechanics, and loading
pretrained weights teaches you how to apply existing models to real problems.

---
title: 2.1 — Understanding word embeddings
description: Why neural networks need numbers, and how words get turned into continuous vectors they can actually learn from.
---

Deep neural networks — LLMs included — **cannot process raw text directly**. Text is
*categorical* ("this word or that word"), and the mathematical operations inside a neural
network only know how to chew on numbers. So before anything else can happen, every
piece of text needs to be turned into a vector of numbers.

That conversion step has a name: **embedding**.

## What is an embedding?

An embedding is a **mapping from a discrete object** (a word, an image, an entire
document) **to a point in a continuous vector space**. Its job is simple: turn non-numeric
data into a format a neural network can process.

Different kinds of data need different embedding models. A model that embeds text won't
work for audio or video. Within text itself there are different granularities:

- **Word embeddings** — one vector per word. The most common kind.
- **Sentence embeddings** — one vector per sentence or paragraph. Popular for
  *retrieval-augmented generation* (RAG), where the model pulls relevant passages from an
  external knowledge base before answering.
- **Document embeddings** — one vector per whole document. Used in search and clustering.

For our purposes — training a GPT-like LLM that generates text one word at a time —
**word embeddings are the focus**.

## Where do word embeddings come from?

The earliest popular approach was **Word2Vec**, which trained a small neural network to
predict a word from its neighbours (or vice versa). The big idea behind Word2Vec:

> Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

When you project those vectors into 2D for visualisation, words with similar meanings
naturally cluster together — birds near other birds, cities near other cities, and so on.

## Dimensions and what they buy you

Word embeddings can have anywhere from 1 to thousands of dimensions.

- **More dimensions** = room to capture more nuanced relationships, at the cost of more
  compute.
- **Fewer dimensions** = faster and easier to visualise, but coarser.

For real models:

| Model | Embedding size |
|---|---|
| GPT-2 small (117M / 125M) | 768 |
| GPT-3 (175B) | 12,288 |

## A key difference vs. pretrained embeddings

You *could* grab a Word2Vec lookup table off the shelf and feed its vectors into your
model. But LLMs don't do that. Instead, they **learn their own embeddings as part of the
model**, starting from random values and updating them during training.

The advantage: the embeddings end up **tuned to the exact task and dataset**. The
disadvantage: you have to actually train the model. That's the trade.

In the rest of this chapter we'll walk through every step needed to produce these
embeddings: splitting text into tokens, turning tokens into integer IDs, converting IDs
into vectors, and finally adding positional information so the model knows *where* each
token sits.
